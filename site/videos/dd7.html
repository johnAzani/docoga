The Hook

My people, wetin dey play for rent increases these days? It's like every other month, your landlord just calls you and says, 'Oga, madam, rent don increase oh!' And before you know it, your budget is sweating. The anger around rent increases is real, and for good reason. With the cost of living skyrocketing, an unexpected or excessive rent hike can throw your entire life off balance. Many tenants feel helpless, like they have no say in the matter. But I'm here to tell you something important: you have rights. And those rights, my friend, depend on where you live. Today, we're diving deep into two major states: Enugu and Lagos, to show you what the law says about how much your landlord can increase your rent.

Enugu's Hard Cap: 15% Max

Let's start with Enugu State, because it has one of the clearest and strongest protections for tenants when it comes to rent increases in Nigeria. This is big news! In Enugu, a rent increase may not exceed fifteen percent of the existing rent, and your landlord can only review the rent after every second anniversary of your tenancy. This is not just a suggestion, it's the law, specifically from the Landlord and Tenant (Amendment) Law 2008. The section explicitly states that the difference between the new and the existing rent shall not exceed 15% of the existing rent. So, if your yearly rent is ₦400,000 from January 2025, your landlord cannot review it before January 2027, and even then, the new rent cannot be more than ₦460,000. This is a clear ceiling, a hard cap on increases for sitting tenants.

Conditions for Enugu's Protection

Now, it's important to understand the conditions under which this Enugu cap applies. The law outlines three key conditions that must all be met. First, the review must be by the parties, meaning both you and your landlord must agree. A unilateral increase, where your landlord just dictates the new rent, is ineffective and not enforceable. Second, the increase cannot happen before the second anniversary of your tenancy. This gives you stability for at least two years. And third, of course, the increase must not be more than fifteen percent above your existing rent. This specific cap applies to a sitting tenancy being reviewed. It does not dictate what a landlord may ask of a brand-new tenant, for whom the rent remains as agreed. So, if you're already living in a property in Enugu, these protections are for you.

Lagos: No Cap, But a Remedy

Now let's switch gears and head over to Lagos, the Centre of Excellence. Here, the situation is different. Unlike Enugu, there is no statutory percentage cap on rent increases in Lagos. You won't find a law saying your rent can only go up by 10% or 15%. This is a crucial point many tenants misunderstand, often confusing Lagos's rules with those of other states or even proposed bills. However, Lagos does offer a powerful remedy for tenants facing what they consider an unreasonable increase. Section 37 of the Tenancy Law 2011 gives a sitting tenant a real way to fight back. It allows you to apply to the Court to have an increase declared unreasonable. This means you have a legal avenue, even without a percentage cap.

Challenging an Increase in Lagos

So, how exactly do you challenge an unreasonable rent increase in Lagos? You start by filling out Form TL11 and applying to the Magistrates’ Court for an order. The Court will then issue a hearing notice on Form TL12 and will seriously consider several factors. They'll look at the general level of rents in your locality, or a similar one, listen to evidence from witnesses, and examine any special circumstances related to your case. If the Court is satisfied that the increase is indeed unreasonable, it may order, on Form TL13, that the increase be changed to a specific amount. What's more, and this is very important for your peace of mind, it is unlawful for your landlord to eject you while your application for an unreasonable increase is pending. This gives you protection during the legal process.

Bills are NOT Law (Yet!)

Across Nigeria, there's a lot of talk about new laws. You've probably heard rumours or seen viral posts about rent caps. The truth is, there is no federal percentage cap on rent increases; the House of Representatives did adopt a motion urging a twenty percent cap, but a resolution is not a law, and it binds no landlord or court. Even in Lagos, while a bill passed second reading in 2025, the Tenancy Law of 2011 remains the law until the Assembly passes the bill and the Governor assents. The same applies in Enugu, where new bills proposing changes are still pending. Until the Governor assents and the law is gazetted, the 2008 amendment on rent review is the newest Enugu law. Always remember, my friend: a bill is not law. We must cite the law as it stands on the shelf, not what is proposed.

New Tenants and Market Rates

It's crucial to distinguish between existing tenancies and new ones. The specific rent increase cap in Enugu and the 'unreasonable' challenge in Lagos are primarily for sitting tenants. For a new tenant, the law generally allows the rent to be 'as agreed' between the landlord and the tenant. This means that a landlord can ask for any amount they deem fit for a vacant property, and your negotiation skills come into play. This is why, with rents reportedly rising 25-40% in Lagos, the standing national mood is one of rent-increase anger. If you are a new tenant, your best protection is a clear, written tenancy agreement that specifies the initial rent and any terms for future increases, so there are no surprises down the line.

Paper Beats Memory: Document It!

Whether you are in Enugu or Lagos, one principle holds true: paper beats memory. Your tenancy agreement is your first line of defence. It should clearly state your starting rent and the terms of your tenancy. In Enugu, if your landlord tries to increase rent before the second anniversary or by more than 15%, you can point to the law and your agreement. If they still push an unlawful increment, a court will not enforce that increased figure. In Lagos, always insist on a rent receipt. Lagos is one of the few states that makes a rent receipt a statutory duty and prescribes what must be on it. This receipt is your proof of payment and of your current rent, which is vital if you need to challenge an increase in court. Always get a receipt for every payment!
