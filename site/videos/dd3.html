The Hook

Show me your receipts. Four words. In a Lagos magistrate's court they end more rent disputes than any lawyer's speech. Here is how it usually goes. A tenant has paid three years of rent, in cash, every year, to the same landlord. Then the landlord wants him out, and the story changes. "You never paid me a kobo this year." And he can say it looking you in the eye, because there are no receipts and no records. It is memory against memory. And in that room, the man holding the paper wins. Not the man shouting louder. In the next ten minutes you will learn what a rent receipt actually is, the seven lines that make one bulletproof, the quiet traps that swallow proof, what the law says, and exactly what to do if years of your rent sit unreceipted. What a Receipt Is

What a rent receipt is, and what it is not. A rent receipt is an acknowledgement of payment. It records that money moved, how much, from whom, to whom, and what for. That is its whole job, and it does that job better than anything else you own. It is not a contract. It does not create your tenancy and it does not set your rights. Your tenancy agreement does that job, and the two documents work together. Now, who can issue a rent receipt? The named landlord, or an agent who has been authorised in writing to collect rent on the landlord's behalf. That is the list. Not the caretaker who happens to be at the gate. Not the son abroad who called you on WhatsApp. Not a stranger with a photocopy of the agreement. If the person collecting your rent cannot show you written authority, your money is at risk the moment it leaves your hand.

Seven Lines of Proof

Seven lines of proof. Count them on your fingers, because a receipt missing any one of them is a receipt a lawyer can pick apart. One, the date. When the money was received. In most rent fights, "when" is the whole argument. Two and three, the parties. The full name of the person paying and the full name of the person receiving, spelled exactly as they appear in your tenancy agreement. Four, the amount, in figures and in words. Never "as agreed". Never a blank. Five, the purpose. The word rent, written down. Not a gift. Not a loan. Not "for the house". Rent. Six, the period covered. Which month, or which year of the tenancy, this payment buys you. Seven, the signature of the person who received the money, with the date beside it. Seven lines. Thirty seconds to write. Years of arguments prevented.

The Quiet Traps

The quiet traps. These are how honest Nigerians lose proof they thought they had. Trap one, the wrong name receipt. Your landlord is Mr Ade. The receipt says "received by Tunde". Who is Tunde? If the landlord denies that Tunde works for him, that paper proves nothing. Trap two, part payment with no paper. You pay half now, half next month, and you take a receipt only for the second half. The first half vanishes. Trap three, the promise. "I will give you the receipt later." Later is where receipts go to die. The money has moved; your leverage has moved with it. Trap four, paying into the agent's personal account. The landlord says "I never received a kobo from you", and strictly speaking he is right. Trap five, the renewal trap. Year one has a receipt because everything was formal. In year two nobody receipts anything, because "we already have an understanding". Year two is exactly the year that goes to court.

The Paper-Trail Family

The paper trail family. Your agreement, your receipts, and your notices are one family, and they protect you together. Think about the claims a landlord can make. He claims arrears: "this tenant owes rent." Your answer is your receipts, dated and matched to each period in the agreement. He claims the tenancy never existed, that you are a squatter or a guest. Your answer is the agreement plus the receipts that show rent flowing under it for years. He claims a different figure, that the rent was higher and you underpaid. Your answer is the year three receipt with the amount written in words. Notice what all of this has in common. The fight is not about shouting. It is about who is holding proof when the room goes quiet.

Three Myths

Three myths, and why each one costs tenants money. Myth one: "I paid in cash, so there is no receipt." Backwards. Cash is exactly when you need a receipt most, because cash leaves no other trail at all. A transfer at least leaves a bank entry; cash leaves nothing but the paper you insist on. Myth two: "Receipts are for businesses, not for a family house." Disputes do not happen at the start of a tenancy when everyone is smiling. They happen at the end, when someone wants the flat back. The receipt from year one is read in year five. Myth three: "The bank record is my receipt." The alert is useful, and keep it. But a bank record proves that money moved. It does not prove what the money was for. "Rent for flat four, January to December" is what settles the argument, and only a receipt, or a written acknowledgement, says that.

Digital Proof

Digital proof, because that is where Nigerian tenants actually live now. First, the transfer. The bank record is strong evidence that money moved on a date, to a named account, in a specific amount. Use it. But pair it with purpose. Second, the message that says what it was for. After every transfer, send a message: "Good afternoon sir, I have sent four hundred and fifty thousand naira, rent for flat four, January to December." Then keep the reply. A reply that says "received" is a written acknowledgement. Third, when you do get a paper receipt, photograph it the same day, front and back, and keep the original somewhere fire and flood cannot reach. Fourth, keep everything in two places: your phone, and an email you sent to yourself, or a folder in the cloud. Proof kept in two places survives one disaster.

What to Do Right Now

What to do right now, even if years of your rent sit unreceipted. Step one, the backfill letter. Write to the person you paid, politely, and ask them to confirm in writing the payments you have made, with the periods they covered. Many landlords will sign it without a second thought. Step two, read the reaction. If they refuse, or stay silent, that refusal is information. It tells you to fix your future fast, because the past is already contested. Step three, from today, never move money again without a receipt. Every payment. Every part payment. Receipted on the day. Step four, draft it yourself. A receipt you drafted and they signed is far stronger than no receipt at all. Bring it with the money. And a word to any landlord watching this. A receipt book costs less than a bottle of water, and it prevents a court season. It protects you from the tenant who claims he paid when he did not. Paper is not suspicion. Paper is peace.
