--- C1 ---
Two hundred thousand naira. That is the caution fee a tenant in Lagos handed over last year, on top of two years' rent. Twelve months later she packed out, left the flat clean, and asked for it back. The answer was a list: management fee, repainting, admin. The money was gone. Here is the thing most tenants never hear. The law does not treat that deposit as the landlord's income. It is your money, held in security against damage you might cause. The tenants who get it back and the tenants who lose it are not luckier or louder than each other. The difference is paperwork. In the next ten minutes you will learn what a caution fee is, the only four things a landlord may lawfully deduct, the receipt that protects you, and the exact steps to take when a refund is refused. --- C2 ---
What a caution fee is, and what it is not. A caution fee, also called a security deposit, is a refundable sum paid at the start of a tenancy. It is not rent. It is not a gift. It is not the landlord's income. It is your money, parked against the day you move out, to cover genuine damage or genuine arrears. If there is nothing to deduct, all of it comes back. How much is normal? Across Nigerian cities the figure commonly sits between five to ten percent of the annual rent, or the value of one to three months' rent. In high-demand areas landlords push harder, and tenants should push back. Ask what the fee covers and when it returns. One more test before you pay. Add up the rent, the agency fee, the legal fee and the caution fee. If the totals don't add up to what you were quoted, or nobody can explain a line, that is not a negotiation. That is a red flag.

--- C3 ---
The rule that decides every refund argument is simple. A landlord may only deduct what the agreement allows and what he can prove. In practice that comes down to four things. One, damage beyond fair wear and tear. Two, unpaid rent. Three, unpaid utilities, like light, water or service charges you agreed to pay. Four, the costs of a breach that was written into the agreement. That is the list. Now the trap. Walls that faded naturally over two years are fair wear and tear. That is the landlord's cost, not yours. A broken window is damage, and that may come out of the deposit. Repainting is not automatic. A landlord repaints between tenants anyway. Unless the walls were actually damaged, it is not your bill. Whatever is deducted must be listed and proved, item by item, naira by naira. A one-line deductions note with no detail is not a deduction. It is a demand.

--- C4 ---
The part most tenants skip is the receipt. Not the rent receipt. A separate one. Your caution fee should be receipted separately, never lumped into the rent figure, because a deposit hidden inside a rent total looks like rent, and rent is not refundable. A proper caution fee receipt has five things. The names of the parties, landlord and tenant, as they appear on the agreement. The property, with the full address. The amount, in figures and in words. The date it was paid. And the refund clause: a sentence saying this sum is refundable at the end of the tenancy, less lawful deductions. That one line is worth more than ten arguments on moving day. Then add a refund deadline. Seven to thirty days after handover is common. Without a date the landlord decides when, and whenever the money is available means never. State the deadline, in writing, before you pay.

--- C5 ---
Build the complete armour on payment day, not on moving day. Five protections, thirty minutes of work. One, the receipt. Caution fee receipted on its own paper. Two, the written refund clause, inside the agreement, not just on the receipt. Three, the refund deadline, a number of days after handover. Four, the move-in condition record. Photograph every room, every wall, every fitting, on the day you collect the keys, and send the photos to the landlord or agent so the date is on record. Five, the itemised-deduction clause: any deduction must be listed in writing, with receipts or quotes, before the balance is paid. None of this is hostile. A serious landlord agrees to all five in a minute, because they protect him too. Thirty minutes of work on day one buys you years of protection afterwards, and the calm of knowing the argument is already settled.

--- C6 ---
Kill the myth. Everyone has heard it. Caution fee is gone money, deposits never come back, just budget for the loss. It is a story told by tenants who lost, and repeated until it sounds like law. It is not law. The law sees the deposit as your money, held in security for the landlord's protection, to be returned when the tenancy ends and the property is handed back in reasonable condition. A landlord who wants to keep any part of it must justify every naira, or refund it all. And the other myth, the aggressive one. A landlord who is owed money does not get to help himself. Seizing a tenant's generator, locking the door, removing the roof, cutting the light: that is self-help, and Nigerian courts have condemned it for decades. In Ihenacho versus Uzochukwu, nineteen ninety-seven, the Supreme Court made it plain. A landlord must recover possession through the court process, not by force. Self-help is trespass. A landlord who seizes property to recover a debt can end up with that debt paid back twice, in reverse: the goods returned, and damages on top.

--- C7 ---
Does your state change the rules? Less than people think. In Lagos, the Tenancy Law of 2011 limits how much rent can be demanded in advance and requires landlords to issue receipts for payments, and a landlord who refuses commits an offence under that law. The deposit itself is still governed mostly by your agreement. You may have heard that a new tenancy bill is moving through the Lagos State House of Assembly. A bill is not law. Do not quote it in a dispute until it is passed and in force. In Abuja, the Recovery of Premises Act governs how possession is recovered, and the procedure protects tenants from being thrown out without process. But on the caution fee question, in Lagos, in Abuja, in Port Harcourt, in Kano, the answer is the same. Look at the agreement and the receipt. Your agreement is the deposit law that matters most. Which is why what you sign on day one is worth more than any argument on the last day.

--- C8 ---
The landlord refuses to refund. Now what? Do not shout, do not threaten, and do not stop paying anything you still owe. Climb the ladder in order. Step one, the written demand. A dated letter or email: the amount, the receipt number, the refund clause, and a deadline of seven to fourteen days. Keep a copy and proof it was delivered. Most refunds happen here, because a landlord who sees a tenant with paper knows how the rest goes. Step two, witnesses and mediation. The agent who introduced you, the estate or landlords' association, a community leader, or the Citizens' Mediation Centre if you are in Lagos. Free, and often enough. Step three, the Magistrates' Court. A caution fee claim is a simple money claim, and you do not need a drama, you need a file. Your exhibits do the talking: the receipt, the agreement, the move-in record, the photographs, the demand letter. Whoever kept the paperwork wins. Not who shouted. Not who suffered.
